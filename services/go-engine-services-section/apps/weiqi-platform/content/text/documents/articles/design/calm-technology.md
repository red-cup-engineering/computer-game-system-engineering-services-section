Audience: designers and engineers building any feature that touches motivation, ratings, or notifications on the weiqi platform.

This document is [Pillar 4 — Calm by construction](./design-pillars.md#pillar-4--calm-by-construction) worked out in full: the register of what we refuse and why, the rating system that has to hold Pillar 4's promise under real matchmaking pressure, and an honest account of where retention comes from when discomfort is off the table. Where this document commits a later phase to something, it says so in [Roadmap obligations](#roadmap-obligations) at the end.

The oath, stated once so the rest of the document can cite it rather than repeat it: *the platform manufactures no urgency and no guilt.* Every pattern in Part 1 is refused because it fails one of Pillar 4's three decision tests — would we still build this without engagement numbers, does it create anxiety the player didn't bring, can the player leave for a month and come back to warmth. A feature that fails any of the three does not ship, regardless of what a growth dashboard would say about it.

## Part 1 — The anti-patterns register

These eight patterns are named directly in Pillar 4's text or its forbids list. Naming them here does two things a general principle can't: it makes the refusal auditable against a specific proposal, and it is honest about *why* each pattern is effective — a refusal that pretends the pattern doesn't work is a refusal nobody will trust under launch pressure. Say the mechanism out loud, then say why it doesn't buy what it costs.

| Pattern | Why it works | Why we refuse it | What we do instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Streaks** — a visible daily/weekly play counter that zeroes on a missed day | Loss aversion reframed as an asset: the player isn't chasing a reward, they're defending a number they already feel they own. This is the single largest documented lever in mobile-learning retention design. | Pillar 4 forbids it by name. It manufactures exactly the anxiety the calm oath rules out, and it punishes the players most likely to have an irregular week — parents, shift workers, anyone whose life doesn't run on the app's clock. | The mastery map (Part 3) never regresses. A month-long gap shows the same concept graph, at most a little dimmer, that the player left behind — evidence that fades, never a chain that breaks (the exact mechanism is [Mastery Model](./mastery-model.md#decay-the-map-fades-it-does-not-punish)'s decay rule). |
| **Lives** — a capped attempt count that depletes on failure and must be waited out or bought back | Converts play into a scarcity-managed resource; the player starts optimizing the meter itself (a second-order goal) instead of the content, which is exactly the F2P mechanic it was built to be. | Forbidden by name. Go is a game where losing is the normal path to skill — [How Ranks Work](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../science-services-section/services/philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/board-games-services-section/services/weiqi-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/teaching-articles/references/how-ranks-work.md) teaches that a settled rating should lose close to half its games. A lives system would punish the very behavior the platform exists to normalize. | Unlimited attempts on every tsumego and tutorial position. Counterfactual replay ([Pillar 2](./design-pillars.md#pillar-2--feedback-while-the-decision-is-still-warm)) turns a "failed" attempt into the very next move, not a spent resource. |
| **Expiring rewards** — time-boxed bonuses or badges that vanish if not claimed by a deadline | Recruits anticipated regret: an unclaimed reward starts to feel like an active loss before the deadline even arrives, because loss aversion applies prospectively as well as retrospectively. Documented as a "temporal" manipulation category in mobile dark-pattern research [@niknejad2024levelup]. | Forbidden by name in Pillar 4's own text ("no expiring rewards"). Manufactures urgency where none is needed. | Mastery evidence and teaching standing (Part 3, [Pillar 5](./design-pillars.md#pillar-5--a-place-in-the-lineage)) never expire. The canon library and the cast roster are always there to come back to, on the player's schedule. |
| **Variable-ratio reward boxes** — randomized reward containers on an unpredictable payout schedule | The variable-ratio reinforcement schedule is the most extinction-resistant schedule known in operant conditioning — the same schedule that powers a slot machine. Regulators now treat it as gambling-adjacent for exactly this reason [@ftc2022darkpatterns; @kingdelfabbro2018]. | Forbidden by name. It would also collide with Pillar 5: Go's own forms of standing (teaching credit, club rank) would be displaced by a manufactured currency, which Pillar 5's forbids list rules out directly. | Progression is legible and deterministic. A concept climbs [Mastery Model](./mastery-model.md#the-five-evidence-tiers)'s five fixed tiers — encountered, named, exercised, applied, teaches — each earned from a specific, inspectable semantic event, never a randomized drop. |
| **Loss-aversion notifications** — push copy built around what the player stands to lose by not returning ("your rating is dropping," "you're about to lose your streak") | Same loss-aversion mechanism as streaks, delivered as an interrupt instead of waited for; it turns the OS notification tray into a standing threat surface rather than a message. | Forbidden by name ("notification strategies designed around loss aversion"). Fails the month-away test directly — a player who put the app down should not open their phone to a warning. | Where notifications exist at all, they are informational and player-triggered ("your correspondence opponent moved") — never framed around what ignoring them costs. |
| **Leaderboard-as-default** — a ranked leaderboard surfaced on the home or dashboard screen | Social comparison — proximity to peers who outrank you is one of the most reliable engagement triggers in social software, because upward comparison reads, behaviorally, as motivation even when it's experienced as status anxiety. | Forbidden by name. Also fails Pillar 5's standing test: Go's real standing is teaching lineage and club rank, not a scoreboard the platform invented. | A leaderboard exists (Phase 4, clubs and tournaments) but lives behind explicit navigation, and appearing on it at all requires the player to opt in — see Part 2. |
| **Rating-change ceremony** — an animated or celebratory reveal of the exact rating delta after every game | Exploits the peak-end rule: a vivid emotional spike at the close of an experience disproportionately shapes how the whole session is remembered, so dramatizing the number makes the session more memorable and more re-playable. | Forbidden by name ("rating changes as spectacle"). A Glicko-2 rating is a probability-weighted estimate, not an achievement, and dramatizing it teaches the wrong lesson about what the number means — the opposite of what [How Ranks Work](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../science-services-section/services/philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/board-games-services-section/services/weiqi-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/teaching-articles/references/how-ranks-work.md) is trying to teach. | The rating updates silently in the background. The only place it is visible at all, and only to players who opted in, is the weekly trend widget in Part 2 — plain language, no delta flashed. |
| **Daily-quest guilt** — a checklist of tasks that resets at midnight, with incomplete items shown greyed-out or crossed-out | Combines loss aversion with the Zeigarnik effect (unfinished tasks disproportionately occupy attention) and implementation-intention priming; the list becomes a small daily debt the player feels before they've opened the app. | Forbidden by name. Fails the "would we still build this if we couldn't measure engagement" test directly — a daily quest list has no pedagogical content of its own, only a compliance shape. | The spaced-repetition review queue (tsumego due for review) is a standing invitation, worded as "12 positions worth revisiting," never "12 positions overdue." |

**How to use this register.** A feature proposal that matches a row's *pattern* column does not ship in that form. It is redesigned toward the *instead* column, or it is escalated as a genuine pillar conflict and written up per [design-pillars.md](./design-pillars.md)'s conflict-resolution rule (the earlier-numbered pillar wins, and the tension gets recorded). "We'll make it a softer version of the pattern" is not an escalation; the mechanism named in the middle two columns still fires at a softer intensity, and the psychology doesn't grade on a curve.

## Part 2 — Rating design against rank anxiety

Rank anxiety is not a hypothetical the design is guarding against. It is a documented feature of Go community life: an OGS forum thread asking why players drop out surfaced fear of judgment and fear of defeat as leading, named causes, independent of skill level [@ogsforum2023dropout]. A separate thread has a player describing losing confidence after consecutive losses — "I lose my confidence and play some sort of weird moves that I don't normally play" — with other players' main remedy being to hide their opponent's identity or take a break, not to fix their rating [@ogsforum2024fearplayer]. The rating system below is built to prevent that spiral from starting, not to treat it after the fact.

### Hidden by default

Every account carries one field: `rating_visibility: "private" | "self" | "public"`, defaulting to `"private"` at account creation, with no exceptions and no auto-graduation out of it.

- **`private`** — the Glicko-2 rating exists and is used internally (matchmaking, automatic handicap) but is rendered in no UI the player or anyone else sees.
- **`self`** — rendered in the player's own settings and the weekly trend widget below. Not shown to opponents, not listed anywhere public.
- **`public`** — additionally eligible for the (never home-screen, always opt-in) leaderboard and visible on the player's public profile.

The toggle moves in either direction with zero friction — a plain switch, no confirmation modal, no "are you sure you want to give up your rank" dialog. A confirmation dialog on `public → self` would itself be a miniature rating-change ceremony, which Part 1 already refuses.

This is deliberately one mechanism for two audiences, not a "beginner mode" a player ages out of. A player who has been on the platform for years and never once flips the toggle is not failing to graduate; they are exercising the same right a first-week player has. Competitive players who want their number visible and their name on a ladder are not wrong to want that — Pillar 4 protects players from manufactured anxiety, it does not tell them their own ambition is illegitimate. `public` exists, is one tap away, and carries no judgment either direction.

### What hidden can't hide: automatic handicap

`rating_visibility: "private"` hides the *number*. It does not, and structurally cannot, hide the equivalent signal that automatic handicap broadcasts directly to the opponent in every handicapped ranked game: [How Ranks Work](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../science-services-section/services/philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/board-games-services-section/services/weiqi-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/teaching-articles/references/how-ranks-work.md) states plainly that "the number of handicap stones a weaker player takes from a stronger one is simply the size of the rank gap between them," and the platform plan specs automatic handicap-and-komi from the rating gap for ranked matchmaking. A `private` player paired this way has their rank gap laid out in stones on the shared board, visible to the one person whose judgment an anxious player is most likely to fear — the exact scenario the cited OGS thread describes, where players' actual remedy was to hide their opponent's identity or take a break, not to trust a hidden number [@ogsforum2024fearplayer]. Naming this gap is the fix the earlier version of this section skipped; the honest next step is not pretending `private` closes it, but giving the player a real lever over it.

That lever: any account may additionally set `handicap_broadcast: "automatic" | "even-only"`, defaulting to `"automatic"`. Setting it to `"even-only"` does not hide handicap where it applies — hiding a stone count that's sitting on the board is not something a UI toggle can honestly do — it changes the matchmaker's candidate pool for that player to opponents close enough in rating that [Ritual and Atmosphere](./ritual-and-atmosphere.md#the-nigiri)'s one-grade-gap rule applies (no stones, no komi beyond the even-game table), trading a wider matchmaking pool for never being placed in a game whose handicap stones announce the gap. This is disclosed plainly next to the toggle ("you'll wait longer for a match, and won't play stronger or weaker opponents at a visible handicap") rather than framed as a fix that costs nothing — Pillar 4 asks that a feature be honest about its tradeoff, not that it have none.

### Weekly trend language

For any account with `rating_visibility` at `self` or `public`, the trend widget replaces the raw number with one of five plain-language states, each with a decidable trigger against the account's Glicko-2 rating (R) and rating deviation (RD):

| Trend state | Trigger | Exact copy |
|---|---|---|
| Still settling | RD > 200 (provisional, per [How Ranks Work](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../science-services-section/services/philosophy-services-section/services/relationality-services-section/services/agency-services-section/services/being-services-section/services/doing-services-section/services/playing-services-section/services/gaming-services-section/services/board-games-services-section/services/weiqi-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/teaching-articles/references/how-ranks-work.md)) | "Your rating is still finding its level — expect it to move a lot right now. That's the system learning, not you failing." |
| Rising | trailing-4-week R delta ≥ +1 stone-equivalent, RD ≤ 150 | "You're on a good run — your play is outpacing your rating, and it's catching up." |
| Steady | \|trailing-8-week R delta\| < 0.5 stone-equivalent, RD ≤ 100 | "Your rating has held steady the last few weeks. That's not stalling — it means the number matches your play, which is what a settled rating looks like." |
| Rough week | this-week win rate < 35%, RD ≤ 150 (settled) | "A tougher week. Most players have weeks like this. It evens out." |
| Cooling trend | trailing-4-week R delta ≤ −1 stone-equivalent | "You've lost a bit of ground the past few weeks. Nothing to read into a short stretch like this — take a look at what these games had in common, or just play on." |

No state is ever shown as a raw number by default. A player who wants the raw R and RD can find them one tap further in, under `self`-level settings — available, not withheld, just not the first thing shown.

### Learning games that never count

Every game record carries `rated: boolean`. `rated: false` for: capture-go onboarding games, any game against a Learn-phase cast bot flagged `teaching: true` ([Pillar 3](./design-pillars.md#pillar-3--opponents-are-teachers-wearing-masks)'s first two masks), teaching-game mode (free branch exploration, specified in [Lineage and Clubs](./lineage-and-clubs.md#2-teaching-game-mode)), analysis-board sessions, and every tsumego attempt. `rated: true` only for a matched Play-phase game where both players explicitly entered ranked queue.

**Schema note, corrected.** An earlier draft of this paragraph claimed `rated` was the same field [Integrity and Sanctioned Assistance](./integrity-and-assist.md) uses for assisted-play lanes. That was wrong, and this document defers to that one as the canonical schema: `Game.assist_mode: "none" | "centaur-study" | "engine-transparent"` is its own, separate, three-valued field, not a reuse of `rated` — and a `centaur-study` session isn't a `Game` record at all per that document's Part 2, so it has neither field to begin with. What's actually true, and what a builder should implement, is two independent facts on any real `Game` record: `assist_mode` says what kind of engine consultation the game permitted; `rated` says whether it counts toward Glicko-2. An `engine-transparent` game is a `Game` record with `assist_mode: "engine-transparent"` **and** `rated: false` — both fields set, for related but distinct reasons (assistance was consulted; there's no unassisted result to be honest about). The calm oath only needs `rated`, and only needed this paragraph to stop overclaiming a shared field it doesn't have.

Unrated games still produce full semantic-event feedback and post-mortems (Pillar 2) — the calm oath withholds the *number*, never the *lesson*. A learner gets everything Pillar 2 promises from a capture-go game except a rating delta, because a rating delta on a learning game would be measuring the wrong thing anyway: it would be scoring a lesson, not a match.

### Loss-streak protection

The matchmaker tracks `consecutive_rated_losses` per player, reset to 0 on any rated win. It intervenes at two thresholds, and — unlike an earlier draft of this section, which left the first threshold silent — both are now disclosed:

- **At 3 consecutive rated losses**: for the player's next 2 rated matches, the matchmaker biases its opponent search toward the lower half of the player's rating-uncertainty band (candidates near R − RD/2 or below) rather than sampling symmetrically. Every candidate is still drawn from *inside* that player's own Glicko-2 uncertainty band — the same band the rating system already treats as a fair match for them — so the bias changes which half of an already-legitimate window gets sampled, not whether the resulting game is a legitimate one. The player is told, once, plainly, the first time it fires per streak: a single line on the pre-game screen for those two matches only, at the same visual register as the byo-yomi and no-demotion-ceremony copy elsewhere in this document — "The next couple of matches are drawn from a steadier part of the pool." No modal, no threshold number explained, no persistent badge afterward. A player who doesn't want this can turn it off entirely: a plain `matchmaking_assist: "on" | "off"` switch lives in the same settings row as `rating_visibility`, defaulting to on.
- **At 5 consecutive rated losses**: ranked auto-queueing pauses. The next matchmaking action shows a plain three-button screen with no urgency language and no warning color: *play a practice game against a cast opponent matched to a concept your recent post-mortems have flagged*, *play an unrated casual game*, or *queue for ranked anyway*. All three are one tap.

Three refusal conditions bound this design. First, the protection never changes the player's displayed or effective rating — it only shifts opponent sampling for a handful of games, drawn from a band the rating system already calls fair, and it is always declinable via the toggle above. This is the specific distinction that keeps it from being the failure mode an OGS forum thread on sandbagging documents, where a rank that can be manipulated downward stops meaning anything for the players who encounter it honestly [@ogsforum2026sandbag]: sandbagging is undisclosed and unilateral; this is disclosed, bounded to two games, and switchable by the player it affects. A silent version of this mechanism — which is what shipped in an earlier draft of this section — would have been the sandbagging failure mode wearing a kinder face regardless of good intent; the fix is disclosure and consent, not a better argument for staying quiet about it. Second, at 5 losses the default choice is a screen, not a redirect — the platform proposes a change of pace; it does not make one for the player. Third, on the opponent side of the exchange: because every rated player eventually strings together a 3-loss stretch somewhere in a normal Glicko-2 trajectory, the population supplying "steadier" opponents to a player mid-streak is drawn from the same population that draws on that same courtesy the next time the roles reverse — this is a mechanism players rotate through, not a standing subsidy one class of players pays into on behalf of another. That claim is a design assumption, not yet a measured one; it is exactly what the falsification path below is for.

Whether any of this is actually working — whether it lowers anxiety rather than just looking like it should — is not this document's claim to settle. [Playtest Protocol](./playtest-protocol.md#rank-anxiety-pulse) specifies the instrument, and the pulse is now wired to this specific mechanism rather than left to lean on the ambient population trend alone: every pulse response is tagged, on-device, with whether the respondent crossed the 3-loss or 5-loss threshold in the 7 days before being asked, so a release can compare threshold-crossers' answers against non-crossers' in the same window instead of reading one blended trend line. See [Playtest Protocol's rank-anxiety pulse](./playtest-protocol.md#rank-anxiety-pulse) for the split and its honest limits (no randomization, and a solo-dev install base rarely clears the N this kind of comparison really wants). That cohort split, not the raw population trend, is this section's falsification path.

### No demotion ceremony

The decidable rule: no UI element — toast, modal, animation, sound, or color change — may trigger conditionally on `rating_delta < 0`. Not a softened version of the rating-change ceremony refused in Part 1; none at all. Where the platform marks the end of a game with ritual (the bow, per Pillar 5), that ritual is identical regardless of who won, because it is closing the game's magic circle, not scoring it.

## Part 3 — The honest retention theory

Pillar 4 names three honest sources of motivation — visible mastery, story, belonging — and forbids any retention case that rests on discomfort. This section takes that seriously as an empirical claim, not just a value statement: each source below is a specific product surface with a specific, falsifiable prediction about player behavior, the kind of claim a playtest should be able to confirm or kill.

**What these predictions are, honestly.** All five below are correlational cohort comparisons, computed from session logs, not randomized experiments. A solo-dev shop has no practical way to run a randomized holdout on its own retention features — withholding the mastery map from a random slice of players to get a clean control group would itself cost those players something for the sake of measurement, which Pillar 4's own first decision test ("would we still build this if we could not measure engagement") argues against doing gratuitously. So each prediction below is stated with the specific confound it cannot rule out, rather than presented as cleaner evidence than it is; [Playtest Protocol](./playtest-protocol.md#mechanism-specific-return-metrics) carries the actual collection design and the same honesty about small-N limits it applies everywhere else.

**The mastery map.** The concept graph filling in with recorded evidence — climbing [Mastery Model](./mastery-model.md#the-five-evidence-tiers)'s five tiers, encountered through named, exercised, applied, and teaches — satisfies the competence need that self-determination theory identifies as one of the two strongest drivers of sustained motivation without external pressure [@ryanrigby2006]. The pull here is not "don't lose progress," it's "watch the map get more filled in" — a fundamentally different feeling from a streak, because a tier once reached is never taken away, only dimmed by absence. *Testable prediction:* sessions in which a player views their own concept-graph page should predict a higher rate of session-initiation-without-any-notification within the following 7 days, compared to matched sessions that didn't include a concept-graph view. *What this can't rule out:* players who are already more engaged are also more likely to check a dashboard and more likely to come back regardless of the dashboard — a selection effect, not proof the map caused the return. The stronger, still-not-randomized version of this test is within-player: does the same player's post-session return rate change after their *first* concept-graph view compared to their own baseline before it, which at least controls for who the player already was.

**The cast.** Opponents with a legible style and a named flaw ([Pillar 3](./design-pillars.md#pillar-3--opponents-are-teachers-wearing-masks)) let a player form an intention more specific than "play again" — "beat the one who won't let go of dead stones." This is Sutton-Smith's rhetoric of the imaginary doing real motivational work: the opponent is a character, not a difficulty number. *Testable prediction, revised to actually risk being wrong:* a session that ends with a "rematch this opponent" action, tagged to a specific cast member, should show a *higher* same-day-or-next-day return rate than a session that ends with a **same-difficulty, anonymous "play again" action against an unnamed opponent of matched strength** — not against a generic "find another game" action. The original comparison was close to unfalsifiable: choosing any specific continuation over a generic one is already a stronger intent signal, so it would predict a higher return rate almost by construction, regardless of whether the character framing was doing anything. Holding difficulty constant and varying only "named cast member" versus "anonymous, same strength" isolates the thing Pillar 3's design actually claims — that the *character* is doing motivational work, not merely that a specific next step beats a vague one. A null result here is a real failure for the doctrine, not just for the metric.

**Your-games-textbook.** Post-mortems (Pillar 2) turn a player's own game history into their own curriculum — the player is the protagonist of the material they're studying, which is Sutton-Smith's rhetoric of self operating on the platform's own data rather than borrowed content. *Testable prediction:* sessions that open the player's own annotated game history should show a higher rate of a subsequent, voluntary analysis-board session within 48 hours than the population baseline. *What this can't rule out:* the same selection effect as the mastery map — a player already inclined to study is more likely to open both surfaces.

**Lineage.** Teaching credit and a visible teacher/student tree ([Lineage and Clubs](./lineage-and-clubs.md#1-lineage-as-a-graph), operationalizing [Pillar 5](./design-pillars.md#pillar-5--a-place-in-the-lineage)) give a form of standing that is not a number and does not decay — the opposite of everything refused in Part 1, and explicitly "recognition, never currency" in that document's own words. *Testable prediction:* players with at least one `confirmed` lineage edge as teacher should show longer platform tenure (weeks with at least one session) than matched non-teaching players at the same rating band, holding games-played roughly constant. *What this can't rule out:* players who stick around long enough to be asked to teach are, by construction, the players who were already going to stay longer — tenure may be a precondition for teaching status as much as a consequence of it. [Lineage and Clubs](./lineage-and-clubs.md#15-abuse-considerations) is honest about a version of this same tension in its own "nothing to farm" claim; this prediction is where that tension becomes a number, not just an argument.

**Canon.** Classic games playable as documentaries invite return out of curiosity about a four-thousand-year-old culture, not obligation to a system. *Testable prediction:* sessions that begin from a canon-game entry point should disproportionately belong to accounts with a 7-or-more-day gap since their last session, compared to sessions beginning from matchmaking or the lesson queue — evidence that canon content is doing the specific job of pulling a lapsed player back, which no urgency mechanic was allowed to do instead.

Each prediction above is written so a playtest can falsify it: a null result on any one of them means that surface isn't doing the retention work Pillar 4 is counting on it for, and it should be redesigned or the claim retracted — not kept on faith because it's the calm option. None of the five is a randomized-controlled result and none should be reported as one; "correlational, with a named confound" is the honest ceiling for what a solo operator's logs can prove here, and [Playtest Protocol](./playtest-protocol.md#mechanism-specific-return-metrics) is written to that ceiling rather than past it.

## Open questions for the board

Two gaps surfaced while writing this document live in [Design Pillars](./design-pillars.md) itself, not in anything below it, so per that document's own instruction they are recorded here rather than silently edited into the constitution:

- **Pillar 4's first decision test — "would we still build this feature if we could not measure engagement?" — has no auditable check.** Every other pillar's decision tests point at an observable fact a third party could verify (ten seconds to first placement, a stated glossary-grounded lesson, a describable opponent flaw). This one asks a designer to introspect on a counterfactual motive that no reviewer can independently confirm, and that any proposal's author can sincerely answer "yes" to without contradiction. This document tries to compensate by making Part 1's refusals concrete and by giving Part 3's retention predictions falsifying evidence rather than resting on the test alone, but that is a workaround, not a fix to the test itself.
- **No pillar's decision tests check for cross-feature signal leakage**, and this document is proof the gap is real, not hypothetical: `rating_visibility: "private"` passes Pillar 4's tests in isolation, and automatic handicap passes Pillar 5's tests in isolation, but the second re-exposes exactly the rank-comparison signal the first exists to hide (see "What hidden can't hide," above). The tie-break rule ("the conflict gets written down") also has no check that would catch a document that never notices it has a conflict to write down in the first place — [Opponent Cast](./opponent-cast.md#where-pillar-3-meets-pillar-4) names a concrete instance of exactly that failure mode inside its own doctrine section.

## Roadmap obligations

This document commits the following to later phases, per [design-pillars.md](./design-pillars.md#how-the-pillars-bind-the-roadmap)'s instruction that Phase 2 "must arrive with Pillar 4's rating posture built in, not retrofitted":

- **Phase 2 (Play)** ships `rating_visibility` defaulting to `"private"`, `handicap_broadcast` defaulting to `"automatic"`, and `rated: boolean` on every game record from the very first commit that introduces ratings — not added after a launch in which everyone's rating was public by default. The loss-streak protection thresholds (3 / 5) and the `matchmaking_assist` toggle are implemented in the matchmaker's opponent-sampling logic before ranked queueing is exposed to any user, not patched in after the first forum thread about tilt, and the pre-game disclosure line for the 3-loss threshold ships in the same commit as the threshold logic itself — a disclosed mechanism with the disclosure missing is not this document's design. This is the same "not retrofitted" commitment [Playtest Protocol](./playtest-protocol.md#roadmap-obligations) already makes for the rank-anxiety pulse's consent banner — the two ship together, since the pulse has nothing to measure until this section's rating design exists to be measured.
- **Phase 3 (Understand)** ships the concept graph as a first-class, player-facing mastery-map page — not only backing data for the legibility layer, per [Mastery Model](./mastery-model.md#ui-stance) — because Part 3's first testable prediction depends on players being able to view it at all.
- **Phase 4 (Belong)** relies on teaching-credit lineage tracking (the `confirmed` edge, the teacher/student tree — already specified in [Lineage and Clubs](./lineage-and-clubs.md#1-lineage-as-a-graph)) existing before any leaderboard or tournament feature, so that standing (Pillar 5) is already present on the platform before ranking (Part 2's `public` visibility, McMahon pairing) gives players a second, narrower way to be compared.
- **[Playtest Protocol](./playtest-protocol.md#mechanism-specific-return-metrics) now carries Part 3's five predictions**, resolving what an earlier draft of this document left as an open-ended commitment with no forcing function. Each of the five is phase-gated to the release in which its underlying surface actually ships (mastery-map view, the cast's rematch action, own-game review, a confirmed lineage edge, a canon entry point) rather than left to an undated "before any release claims..." condition — see that section for the exact gate per prediction. A release may not cite one of Part 3's paragraphs as a validated retention source until its specific row in that table has collected at least one release-over-release comparison, cohort-split and confound-labeled per this document's Part 3.
